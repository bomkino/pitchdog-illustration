## Atomic grammar

Build the idea from four parts:

1. **Specific actor:** the exact person, dog, team, or object.
2. **Ordinary act:** one familiar verb the actor could naturally perform.
3. **Familiar thing:** one object or simple form the viewer recognizes immediately.
4. **Impossible behaviour:** one physical rule changes and reveals an emotional truth.

Working sentence:

> `[Actor] [ordinary verb] [familiar thing], and the thing [one impossible behaviour].`

The emotional truth belongs underneath the physics, not beside it as another symbol.

## Metaphor-field method

Write eight to twelve compact verbal thumbnails before generating a new
concept. Ten is the strong default. Stop early only when the remaining entries
would be padding and restart the search from a new territory; expand beyond ten
when the field is genuinely distinct. For each, record:

- candidate caption;
- visible relationship in one sentence;
- ordinary act;
- impossible behaviour;
- likely failure;
- keep, reserve, or kill.

Use [metaphor thumbnail template](../templates/metaphor-thumbnails.md).

Generate no image until one premise survives.

### Kill quickly

Kill a thumbnail when:

- the caption is more vivid than the picture;
- the picture needs arrows, labels, text, or a sequence;
- it contains more than one changed physical rule;
- it needs scenery to explain stakes;
- it is a familiar inspirational icon: puzzle piece, sprout, finish line, lightbulb, ladder, target, key, handshake, mountain summit;
- it treats the subject as decoration beside the idea;
- it turns care into generic cuteness or courage into domination;
- it repeats another image's object, verb, physics, or emotional ending;
- it cannot become one remembered silhouette;
- it would remain generic after changing the subject's name.

## Selection tests

Keep a premise only when all answers are yes:

1. Can a stranger describe the visible event in two seconds?
2. Is the impossibility material rather than rhetorical?
3. Does the actor's ordinary action belong to this actor?
4. Does the relationship reveal a specific emotional truth?
5. Can it fit in one tiny lower-field motif?
6. Can the image work with no text?
7. Is it distinct from every nearby illustration?
8. Does it feel inevitable after being seen, but not obvious before?

## Productive physical turns

Use these as mechanisms, never recipes:

| Physical turn | Useful question |
| --- | --- |
| Weight | What becomes carryable, floating, or shared? |
| Material | What solid thing can fold, unravel, soften, or be mended? |
| Obedience | What unruly thing can sit, wait, listen, or make room? |
| Timing | What useful object arrives before the problem? |
| Containment | What vast or loud thing can fit somewhere humane? |
| Continuity | What break can meet, bridge, stitch, or become a hinge? |
| Scale | What tiny act can alter a large thing without spectacle? |
| Direction | What falls upward, opens sideways, or returns differently? |
| Attention | What supposedly mute or negligible thing receives time? |
| Care | What hard event gets a chair, shoe, pillow, shelter, or handle? |

Do not use the same turn repeatedly across a series.

## Failure families

### Literal service diagram

The image demonstrates a workflow rather than discovering a truth. Repair by removing process steps until one relationship carries the job.

### Caption-first poetry

The sentence is lovely; the image is a dog with a stick. Repair by describing the impossible physics without the caption. If it disappears, kill it.

### Symbol stack

Cloud + bridge + thread + heart + arrow means the artist did not choose. Keep one familiar thing and one changed rule.

### Power fantasy

Actor defeats, crushes, or dominates an obstacle. Prefer composure, anticipation, care, attention, repair, or practical wit unless force is the honest truth.

### Sentimental rescue

Small vulnerable object exists only to make the actor lovable. Give both sides agency or choose a less manipulative relationship.

### Surreal decoration

Impossible object is visually strange but emotionally empty. Ask what material condition changed and why it matters.

### Phrase illustration

The image merely literalizes an idiom. A phrase can start the search, but the final relationship needs a fresh physical turn.

## Caption discipline

Write caption after the visible relationship is locked.

- Keep it one sentence.
- Name the emotional turn without explaining the picture.
- Prefer specific verb and noun.
- Avoid forced dog language, slogans, uplift, and stacked metaphor.
- Do not place caption text inside the image.
- Run the cover test: hide caption; image still works.

Approved captions are calibration evidence, not reusable syntax. Do not produce a series where every line is `[Name] gave X somewhere to Y` or `[Name] taught X to Y`.

## Series variation

Track these axes across a series:

- ordinary verb;
- familiar object;
- changed physical rule;
- silhouette direction;
- number of actors;
- field colour;
- accent role;
- emotional temperature;
- motif position;
- caption mechanism.

Use these axes as a diagnostic. Adjacent images should change enough material
and emotional structure to feel authored, but do not satisfy a numerical quota
with superficial variation. If two images share the same object, verb, physics,
silhouette, and ending, one must change.

For groups, the relationship must distribute work and attention visibly. A three-person or three-dog concept needs three real points of agency, not one hero plus witnesses.
